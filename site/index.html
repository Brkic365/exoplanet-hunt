One star, five stages

From raw pixels to a verdict

Scroll through one star's real data as the pipeline sees it. Pick a known planet, one of the lone dips from sector 105, or any glowing star on the map.

TOI-1194.01

1 · Ingest

Raw light curve

MIT Quick-Look Pipeline photometry from MAST: the star's brightness every 200 seconds over one TESS sector, about 27 days. Cadences flagged for momentum dumps, scattered light or bad pointing are dropped.

2 · Detrend

Remove the star's own wobble

A sliding biweight filter with a 0.75-day window traces spots, rotation and spacecraft drift (the gold line), then divides it out. The window is wide enough to leave a transit of up to about 6 hours intact; injection tests chose it over 0.5, 1 and 1.5 days.

3 · Search

Try every period

A Box Least Squares search written from scratch folds the light curve at each trial period and scores the best box-shaped dip. The same algorithm runs as a CUDA kernel, one thread block per star and period. Lone dips get their own detector: a box slid along the light curve, scored against the star's own red noise.

4 · Fold

Stack the transits

Folding at the best period stacks every transit on top of each other, so a dip too faint to see once becomes clear. The fold uses a light curve re-detrended with the transits masked, so the filter cannot eat the dip being judged.

5 · Vet and classify

Planet, or impostor?

Automatic checks look for the usual impostors: odd and even transits of different depth, a secondary eclipse, light moving toward a neighbouring star. A CNN on the folded views and a gradient-boosted model on the vetting numbers, trained on 2,578 ExoFOP dispositions, give the score.

Write-up

What worked, what did not

Worked

  • The search is at the noise limit. Injected planets and known TOIs follow the same recall curve; the shallow planets it misses are too faint for one sector, not discarded by the pipeline.
  • A box is almost as good as a transit model. The own BLS matches Transit Least Squares on the same 5,000 injections at the same false-alarm rate (26.2% vs 26.0%), and the CUDA version is 41× faster on the same laptop.
  • Vetting numbers beat a bigger network. The hand-built checks lift AUC from 0.78 to 0.93; the CNN on folded light curves alone reaches 0.84.
  • The centroid check earns its place. The strongest periodic signal in sector 105 looked like a 5 R⊕ planet; the centroid moved 22σ toward a neighbour that is an eclipsing binary in older sectors. The classifier gave it 0.07.

Did not, or not yet

  • Recent sectors are rough. 90% of the 8,693 sector-105 detections were sector-wide systematics: orbit-start ramps and a noisy second orbit. Only a cross-star filter removed them.
  • An eclipsing binary's secondary looks like a second planet. The first version masked "other signals" before vetting and hid the very secondaries it should catch.
  • Labels limit the classifier. ExoFOP dispositions teach planet vs astrophysical false positive, not signal vs noise. Adding a noise class helped, but low SNR still needs a hard gate, and lone dips are judged by hand.
  • Not checked: the T16 and transformer-search candidate tables, which were unreachable from the cloud environment. None of the 21 hand-vetted signals needed them.
